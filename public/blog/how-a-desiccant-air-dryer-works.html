    How a heatless desiccant air dryer works

    By Win Equipments engineersUpdated 7 October 20266 min read

    Short answer

    A heatless desiccant dryer has two towers filled with desiccant. Wet compressed air rises through the tower that is on line, the desiccant adsorbs the water vapour, and dry air leaves at the top. A small part of that dry air is expanded down the other tower to regenerate it. A timer then swaps the towers.

    A refrigerated dryer stops at a +3 °C dew point. When your process needs air drier than that, a heatless desiccant dryer removes the water vapour with a bed of desiccant instead. It has two towers, so one is always drying while the other is being regenerated.

    You can turn this dryer, cut it open and follow the air through it in the 3D model further down this page.

    Two towers, one always drying

    Each tower is a pressure vessel filled with a bed of desiccant: activated alumina or molecular sieve. Desiccant adsorbs water vapour onto its surface, much as a sponge holds water, but it can only hold so much. So the dryer works in turns:

    • The tower that is on line dries your compressed air.
    • The other tower is regenerated, which means its desiccant is dried out again so it can be used next.

    "Heatless" means the regeneration uses a small flow of dry purge air instead of a heater, so there is no heater to maintain.

    The cycle, step by step

    1. Wet air in. Compressed air enters at the bottom and rises into Tower A, which is on line.
    2. Adsorb. As the air rises, the desiccant adsorbs the water vapour. The wetted zone of the bed creeps up the tower. Dry air leaves at the top and goes on to your plant.
    3. Purge. At the same time, a small part of that dry air is expanded down Tower B. It carries the moisture out of B's bed and leaves through the purge exhaust and a muffler.
    4. Repressurise. Before the swap, the purge exhaust closes and Tower B refills with dry air, so the change-over does not disturb the air going to your plant.
    5. Switch. The timer operates the valves and the towers swap duties.
    6. Swapped. Now Tower B dries the air while Tower A is purged. Then the cycle repeats.

    The WHD catalogue lists a 10-minute cycle time. Steps 4 and 5 are the general heatless-dryer sequence; the exact valve sequence and timings of a given model are set by its timer.

    Parts of a heatless dryer, in 3D

    The diagrams and animation come from a 3D model built from photographs of a Win WHD dryer. The outside follows the photographs; the bed, the screens and the layout behind the control door are illustrations, because the inside is not photographed. Numbers match the labels.

    1. Controls. A control box on the upper rail has a POWER ON lamp, a DRYER ON lamp and a switch. The WHD catalogue lists a motorised cam timer, with a PLC and cycle-failure indication as options.
    2. Towers. Two welded steel pressure vessels, labelled TOWER-1 and TOWER-2 on the unit, each with a pressure gauge and a pressure relief valve.
    3. Desiccant bed. A large bed of bulk desiccant with enough contact time, and no special cartridges. In the model it is held between perforated support screens; the screens are an illustration.
    4. Air manifolds. Flanged pipework joins the two towers. In this illustration the top header carries dry air and the base pipe brings wet air in.
    5. Valves and mufflers. Non-lubricated inlet control valves, a purge flow valve to adjust the purge rate, and heavy-duty purge exhaust mufflers.
    6. Frame. A structural steel frame with a floor stand and lifting lugs.
    7. Filter housings. Pre- and after-filters can be factory-mounted. The model shows two plain housings behind the frame.

    The dew point you get

    DesiccantPressure dew pointRated at
    Activated alumina−20 °C38 °C inlet and 7 bar g
    Molecular sieve−40 °C38 °C inlet and 7 bar g

    Choose molecular sieve when your process needs the lower dew point. See the ISO 8573-1 purity classes to match a dew point to a class.

    What goes before and after the dryer

    A typical sequence is: air compressor, aftercooler, wet air receiver, pre-filter and zero-oil filter, desiccant dryer, after-filter, dry air receiver. The pre-filter keeps free water and dust off the bed, the zero-oil filter stops compressor oil coating it, and the after-filter catches desiccant dust before it reaches your equipment.     Is a heatless dryer right for you?

    Typical uses for sub-zero dew points are pharmaceutical, electronics, instrument air, food packaging, paint lines and outdoor air lines in cold conditions. Confirm suitability for your process with our engineers. If +3 °C is dry enough, a refrigerated dryer is usually the simpler choice: read refrigerated vs desiccant air dryers, or how to choose a compressed air dryer.

    Frequently asked questions

    What does heatless mean?

    The off-line tower is regenerated with a small flow of dry purge air instead of a heater, so there is no heater to maintain.

    Why does a desiccant dryer have two towers?

    Desiccant can only hold a limited amount of water. One tower dries your air while the other is regenerated, and a timer swaps them, so dry air is always available.

    Alumina or molecular sieve: which do I need?

    Activated alumina gives a −20 °C pressure dew point and molecular sieve gives −40 °C, both at 38 °C inlet and 7 bar g. Choose molecular sieve when your process needs the lower dew point.

    Why does a desiccant dryer need filters?

    A pre-filter keeps free water and dust off the desiccant, a zero-oil filter stops compressor oil from coating it, and an after-filter catches desiccant dust before it reaches your equipment.

    What working pressure is supported?

    Standard WHD dryers work from 7 to 12.5 bar g with a design pressure of 13.2 bar g. Higher pressures can be offered on request.
