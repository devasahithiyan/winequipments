A heat exchanger moves heat from one fluid to another without letting the two mix. It is one of the most common pieces of process equipment. This guide covers the main types in general, then the shell and tube design in detail.

## Common heat exchanger types

The following is general engineering knowledge.

| Type | Basic idea | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shell and tube | Tube bundle inside a cylindrical shell | Oil coolers, process cooling, condensers |
| Plate | Stack of thin corrugated plates with narrow channels | Clean liquids, compact duties |
| Finned coil (air cooled) | Fluid in finned tubes, air blown across | Dry coolers, radiators |
| Double pipe | One pipe inside another | Small duties |
| Tube-in-tube coil | Coiled tubes nested together | Compact refrigeration equipment |

Each has its place. Shell and tube suits higher pressures and temperatures and is easy to inspect and clean in many designs.

## How a shell and tube exchanger works

One fluid flows through a bundle of tubes. The other flows around the outside of the tubes, inside the shell. Heat passes through the tube wall. Typical parts are:

- **Tubes:** the heat transfer surface.
- **Shell:** the outer cylinder holding the second fluid.
- **Tube sheets:** plates holding the tube ends.
- **Baffles:** plates that make the shell-side fluid cross the tubes, improving heat transfer.
- **Heads and nozzles:** direct the fluids in and out.

Common arrangements include fixed tube sheet, U-tube and floating head designs. They differ in how they handle thermal expansion and in how easily the bundle can be removed for cleaning. General principle only: ask the manufacturer which one fits your duty.

## What to send for sizing

The heat exchanger design depends on these inputs:

1. Both fluids and their properties (water, oil, process fluid)
2. Flow rate on each side
3. Inlet and required outlet temperatures on each side
4. Design pressure and temperature
5. Preferred shell and tube materials
6. Allowable pressure drop and connection sizes

## How the design is worked out

The basic balance is the same for any exchanger: heat given up by the hot fluid equals heat taken by the cold fluid, calculated from flow, specific heat and temperature change. The required surface area then follows from that heat duty, the overall heat transfer coefficient and the temperature difference between the fluids. Allowable pressure drop limits how fast fluids can be pushed through, which in turn sets the tube count and length. This is standard textbook method.

## Choosing between fluids and sides

A common rule of thumb in textbooks is to put the dirtier or more fouling fluid on the tube side, because straight tubes are easier to clean than the shell side, and to put the higher-pressure fluid in the tubes, which are thin cylinders that resist pressure well. Treat this as a starting point only. Corrosion, temperature and the fluid's viscosity can change the decision, and the manufacturer will confirm it once you share the duty.

## Materials

Tube and shell material depends on the fluids. Plain steel suits many oil duties; cooling water and aggressive process fluids may call for copper alloys or stainless steel. We list preferred shell and tube materials among the inputs to send, so state any corrosion concern with your enquiry.
