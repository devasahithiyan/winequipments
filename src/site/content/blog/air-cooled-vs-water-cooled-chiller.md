Every WCP process chiller, from the 0.5 TR WCP 005 to the 20 TR WCP 200, is offered with either an air-cooled or a water-cooled condenser. The refrigeration circuit, the stainless steel tank and the pump are the same. The only question is where the heat goes once it has been taken out of your process water.

## Where the heat goes

A chiller does not destroy heat, it moves it. The evaporator takes heat out of your process water, the refrigeration compressor adds its own work, and the condenser has to throw both away. By a simple energy balance, the heat rejected at the condenser is the cooling load plus the compressor input.

For example, a WCP 050 is rated at 5 TR (about 17.6 kW of cooling, since 1 TR = 3.517 kW) with a 6.0 kW refrigeration compressor at maximum rated power. Its condenser must reject up to about 23.6 kW, whichever type you choose.

- An **air-cooled condenser** is a finned coil with fans that blow plant air across it. The heat goes straight into the surrounding air. The WCP model table lists the cooling fans, from 1 on the smaller models up to 8 on the WCP 200.
- A **water-cooled condenser** passes cooling water through the condenser instead. That water carries the heat away to a cooling tower, which rejects it to the atmosphere by evaporation and returns the water at wet bulb + 4 °C.

## Side by side

| | Air-cooled WCP | Water-cooled WCP |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heat rejected to | Plant air around the chiller | Cooling water, then a cooling tower |
| Condenser medium temperature | Dry bulb (ambient) air | Tower water, rated at wet bulb + 4 °C |
| Extra equipment | None | Cooling tower, condenser water pump and piping |
| Water use | None | Tower evaporation, drift and blow-down |
| Where it sits | Needs free air around it; hot air must escape | Can sit indoors; the tower goes outside |
| Capacity range | 0.5 to 20 TR | 0.5 to 20 TR |

## Why site ambient matters

WCP capacity is rated at **15 °C water outlet** and **40 °C ambient** with a 5 °C water ΔT. The catalogue gives these ambient correction factors:

| Ambient | Factor |
| --- | --- |
| 30 °C | 1.2 |
| 35 °C | 1.1 |
| 40 °C | 1.0 |
| 45 °C | 0.9 |
| 50 °C | 0.8 |

An air-cooled condenser rejects heat directly to that air, so a hot plant room cuts into its capacity. A WCP 100 (10 TR) in a 45 °C location delivers about 9 TR; at 50 °C about 8 TR. If your summer peak is 45 °C, size for 45 °C, not for the annual average.

A water-cooled condenser sees tower water instead. Because evaporation cools water toward the wet bulb temperature, which is below the dry bulb air temperature, the condenser usually works against a cooler medium. That is basic psychrometrics, not a promise of a particular saving; send us your tower water temperature and we will rate the chiller for it.

The outlet temperature you need matters too: the same chiller is rated at 0.6 × capacity for 5 °C water and 1.3 × for 25 °C water. Use the [chiller tonnage calculator](/engineering-tools/chiller-tonnage-calculator.html) to apply both factors.

## Does your plant already have a cooling tower?

This is often the deciding question.

- If you already run a WCT tower for moulds, compressors or another process and it has spare capacity, a water-cooled chiller can share it. You add the chiller's condenser heat (the load plus compressor power, as above) to the tower's duty.
- If you have no tower, a water-cooled chiller means buying, installing and maintaining one, with its own pump, piping, water treatment and make-up water. For a single small chiller, an air-cooled unit is usually the simpler package.

To check whether an existing tower can take the extra load, use the [cooling tower calculator](/engineering-tools/cooling-tower-calculator.html) and read [range, approach and wet bulb](/blog/cooling-tower-range-approach-wet-bulb.html).

## Water availability

A cooling tower loses water. The catalogue formula is evaporation loss = 0.00085 × 1.8 × flow (m³/hr) × ΔT. A tower circulating 30 m³/hr over a 5 °C range evaporates about 0.23 m³/hr, before drift and blow-down. Where water is scarce, costly or hard, an air-cooled chiller avoids that make-up water and the scaling that comes with it.

## Space and placement

An air-cooled chiller has to breathe. Give it free space on the condenser side so it does not suck back its own hot discharge air, and do not shut it in a closed room. A water-cooled chiller can sit in an enclosed room close to the process, because its heat leaves through the water pipes; the tower then needs an open, ventilated location outside. Round and square towers suit different plots: see [round vs square cooling towers](/blog/round-vs-square-cooling-towers.html).

## Choose air-cooled when

- You have no cooling tower, or it has no spare capacity
- Water is scarce, costly or poor in quality
- The chiller can stand where hot air escapes freely
- You want one self-contained package with the fewest parts to maintain

## Choose water-cooled when

- A cooling tower with spare capacity already serves the plant
- The chiller must sit indoors or in a room without good ventilation
- Your plant room runs very hot and you would lose air-cooled capacity to the ambient correction
- You have reliable make-up water and a water treatment routine
