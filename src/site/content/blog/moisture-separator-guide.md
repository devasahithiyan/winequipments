A moisture separator is a vessel that removes liquid water from compressed air. Hot air leaving a compressor carries most of its water as vapour. Once an aftercooler cools it, much of that vapour condenses into droplets, and the separator catches them so they can be drained away.

## What it does and does not do

A separator removes **liquid** water and some oil droplets. It does not remove water **vapour**. The air leaving a separator is still saturated at its new, lower temperature. Vapour is the job of a dryer, so a separator is a first stage, not a replacement.

## How it works

This is the standard principle used by separators in general:

1. Cooled air carrying droplets enters the vessel.
2. The air is forced to change direction, often with a swirl, or is slowed down.
3. Droplets, being heavier than air, are thrown against the wall or fall out.
4. Water collects at the bottom and is discharged through a drain.

## Where it sits

The separator goes straight after the aftercooler, before the receiver and the dryer:

compressor, aftercooler, moisture separator, receiver, filters and dryer

[Aftercoolers with moisture separator](/products/industrial-aftercoolers.html) cool the compressor discharge air so the bulk of its water condenses and drains out before the receiver and dryer. ## The drain matters

A separator is only as good as its drain. If the drain sticks closed, the vessel fills and water carries over into the line. If it is left open it wastes air. Fit an [automatic drain valve](/products/automatic-drain-valves.html), which our catalogue lists for moisture separators. See the [drain valve guide](/blog/automatic-drain-valve-guide.html) for the types.

## Why it matters downstream

Water that passes the separator ends up in the receiver, filters and pipework. Standing water corrodes steel pipes and receivers, washes lubricant out of pneumatic tools and cylinders, and can freeze in cold lines. In a plant with a dryer, excess liquid also overloads the dryer. Taking out the bulk of the liquid at the start of the line is the cheapest water removal in the whole system.

## Signs the separator is not coping

- Water spits from the receiver drain or from air tools
- The dryer's drain runs constantly or its dew point rises
- Filters downstream fill with liquid quickly
- The separator drain is blocked, stuck or never discharges

Check the drain first, because a blocked drain causes most of these. Our [troubleshooting guide](/blog/troubleshooting-common-problems.html) covers dryer-side symptoms.

| --- | --- | --- |
| Main job | Removes bulk liquid water | Removes fine oil and water aerosol |
| Position | After aftercooler | After separator and receiver, ahead of the dryer |
| Removes vapour | No | No |

## Air-cooled or water-cooled

How well the separator works depends on how much the air has been cooled first, because only cooled air has condensed water to give up. The aftercooler page describes air-cooled units and lists air- or water-cooled preference and cooling water availability among the details we ask for. The cooler the air entering the separator, the more water is captured there rather than further down the line.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need a moisture separator if I have a dryer?
It is good practice to have one ahead of the dryer. It removes bulk liquid so the dryer is not flooded with water.

### Where exactly should it be installed?
Immediately after the aftercooler, before the air receiver.

### Why is water still in my air after the separator?
Because the air leaves saturated with vapour. Only a dryer lowers the dew point enough to stop condensation in the lines.
